The Validator Investigation, Explained Simply
— A plain-language summary of the whole research project

Basis: on-chain records as of October 31, 2026. Everything here is a distillation of the detailed reports (linked at the end). Tap (click) any address to open its actual records on the explorer site.

1. The short version

  • This chain has 23 record-keepers (validators). The investigation examined the movements of every one of their wallets.
  • The biggest finding: right before the token "listing" (the moment the general public becomes able to buy), validator X's connected wallets bought in heavily, ahead of the public and at low prices. The officially announced starting price was $0.01, but the first price the public could actually buy at was $0.076 — and that difference went to those who bought early.
  • The X group's assets now sit not with X himself but in the "connected wallets." In particular, the wallet called W5 is effectively the vault.
  • Other validators also show in-step patterns — "holding the same amount of shares," "selling the same number of times" — but it cannot be concluded that the same person is operating them from behind the scenes.
  • Tracing as far as other chains (such as Ethereum) brought into view the overseas exchange MEXC and a "payout-only wallet" whose first funding came from Liquid, a former domestic exchange. Who operates it, though, is not known.

3. What happened on listing day

On June 30, 2026, this chain's two headline tokens, [42] and DONT, were "listed" — the day the general public was supposed to be able to buy them. Here is that day as a diagram.

Before the day (May 25 – June 29)
The exchange pools were built in advance. The price was $0.01 — the same "starting price" the project had announced beforehand. But trading for the public had not begun; only quiet insider tests and the distribution of liquidity material (explained later) took place. On June 23 a pool for the dollar-pegged token (USDT) was also created, and the same day AF's wallet received 1,490 USDT (about $1,490) newly created out of thin air. This became the war chest for the day.
▼ June 30, 13:18 (42 minutes before the listing)
The advance buying begins
Two wallets, A8 and AF, start buying [42] in a pool nobody else is trading in yet. Of the 43 buys between 13:18 and 14:00, 35 were theirs, and the amount they spent was exactly the war chest: 1,490 USDT. They took 62,240 (56.6%) of the pool's 109,900 [42] before the public opening. The price rose from $0.01 to $0.0756. A8 then used some of that [42] to buy DONT 28 minutes before its listing too, and DONT's price was already up +36% before it opened.
▼ June 30, 14:00 (the official start)
Public trading begins
At 14:00:03 the first trade by a member of the public arrived. But by then the cheapest available price was already $0.076. The public never had a single chance to buy at $0.01. At this moment the X group held 0% of the deposit shares (LP) in both pools — the precise picture is not "they controlled the liquidity" but "they skimmed the cheap stock first."
▼ June 30, 15:00
The official "trading has started" announcement
The official account announced the start of trading at 15:00 — one hour after it actually started. By then the price was $0.2962, about 30 times the announced starting price. People who rushed in on the announcement could not even buy at $0.076. The price later reached $0.42 at 17:00 (42 times the starting price).

What the problem is, in plain terms: only the side that appears to have known the starting time could buy cheaply; the public, who did not know, could only grab high prices. That unfairness is the heart of the issue. Note that the distribution of the initial liquidity material to validators is not counted as a problem in this research: for a community-led project that is a normal distribution, and it had been announced in advance. Whether any of this is "illegal" depends on the country and the interpretation of its laws, and this research does not make that call — what the records do establish is the gap between the announcement and the reality, and the absence of any disclosure.

The daily reward (FISH) story

From September 6 to October 31, 2026, X received the reward token FISH every single day for 56 days without a break. The total was 134.7003 FISH (an average of 2.405 per day). About 80% of it (108.8447) was turned into pool deposits, about 13% was burned by the operator's machinery, and only about 6% (7.8584) was sold. His FISH balance is now zero, and what came in matches what went out to the last fraction. No trace was found of him claiming [42] rewards on his stake.

5. How the validators connect to each other

Direct money transfers between validators are very rare: even on the other transfer system (Cosmos sends) there are only 19 directed pairs, and the number of pairs that sent to each other both ways is zero. Even so, several "moving in step" patterns were found. None of them proves the same person is behind the wallets, so they are presented without over-claiming.

6. Buying the very first moment (a "snipe")

Buying the instant a new token starts trading, while it is still cheap, is called a snipe in this research (counted here as any buy within 1 hour of trading starting).

Across the 59 trading pools involving [42], snipe buys by validator-connected wallets numbered 103. The breakdown: validators themselves 0, directly connected wallets 17, and wallets connected only by inference 86 (83%). In other words, no identifiable validator sniped the [42] pools in person.

Even widening the count to every trading pool, a validator's own snipe amounts to exactly one case:

WhoWhat they did
farmer himself (0x46c7…de41)The only in-person validator snipe. Bought 3,758,841 MARVIN about 33 minutes after FISH/MARVIN trading began
Outside fast buyer 0xa6b3…6f9e47 snipes across 27 pools. In one case it bought 151,955,491.7 QOM for 0.1776 [42] at the exact same second (0 seconds) QOM trading opened. Connected to validators by inference only
Outside fast buyer 0xfceb…5553The most active trading wallet on this chain (2,836 trades). Was the very first buyer at the opening bell 3 times. Also inference-only

In short, the main actors in sniping were not the validators but unidentified outside fast buyers (apparently automated programs). What remains on the validator side is farmer's single case and a few by directly connected wallets.

A side observation: for some tokens, the person who created the token holds almost all of it. The issuer of MARVIN, 0x51ff…63dc, received most of what was issued, and the issuer of FOXXEY and BFOXX, 0xaeca…ed30, received 95% of FOXXEY and 100% of BFOXX (42,000,000 each).

Where the money came from, and where it went (diagrams)

Where C8's money comes from
Tracing C8's funds back step by step ends at a personal-trader-style wallet active since 2021 (0x9d51…36bd), whose own money came from ordinary purchases on an exchange (Uniswap) and from withdrawals from the overseas exchange MEXC. MEXC's wallet (0x75e8…cb88) also funded several of C8's companion wallets directly, so this circle clearly used MEXC repeatedly. C8's dollar funding of 1,054.68 also came from a wallet labelled as MEXC (0x9642…5d4e).
▼ A bridge movement that matches to the last digit
C8's bridging is proven
On August 6, 2026, the 577.135946 USDT C8 sent to the bridge on the Ethereum side matches, to the last decimal, the 577.14 USDT created into C8 on this chain. This is a case where "it was carried across the bridge" is backed by amounts, not just guesswork. There were no direct transfers between the five wallets themselves on ETH or BSC.
▼ Where AF's money comes from
AF's activity funds came from a "payout-only wallet"
AF's Ethereum activity began in June 2026, and its first money came from a large unidentified wallet (0xdb3d…58f7). This wallet started in 2019, has 145,561 transactions, and has paid out a total of 181,017 ETH — a pure distribution machine. Its own first funding was 800 ETH from a wallet of the former domestic exchange Liquid (0xdf4b…145b, display name "Liquid 2"). Just before the listing (June 14–19), AF also sent a total of 1,228.24 in dollar funds into another bridge (Mayan). The timing and the carrier line up with the war chest, but it is about 262 short of 1,490, so we do not claim it was the war chest itself.
Who operates that payout wallet (how the debate settled): "the party that first sent it money" is not necessarily its owner. The "Funded By" label only shows who made the first transfer — it is not proof of ownership. In fact, this wallet kept paying out normally in December 2022, while the successor company of its funding source (Liquid) was under a business suspension — so it is considered not to be that company's wallet. A guess circulating online names a domestic exchange, BitPoint, but it remains unconfirmed. The wallet does look like a domestic operator — it holds large amounts of tokens popular in Japan (DEP and JASMY) — but who operates it is undetermined. That is the current conclusion.

10. Glossary

TermPlain explanation
ValidatorThe checker who watches that the chain's records are correct and votes to finalize them. Stakes their own tokens as a bond for the work
WalletA purse for crypto assets. The address starting with 0x works like an account number
Pool (liquidity pool)An "exchange counter" holding two kinds of tokens. The price is set automatically by the ratio of what is inside
Providing liquidity / LP receiptDepositing two kinds of tokens into a pool. The certificate you receive as proof of the deposit (the LP token) is the LP receipt
SwapExchanging one token for another at a pool (a buy or a sell)
SnipeBuying the very first moment trading starts. A move to lock in the cheap price
BridgeMachinery that moves assets to a different blockchain. When assets cross a bridge, the same amount is sometimes newly created on the far side
Staking (the bond)Depositing tokens as the bond for a validator's work. The amount staked decides trust and rewards
Inferred connectionA relationship judged "very likely connected" from matching amounts and timing, without a direct transfer. It is not proof and is never stated as fact
VWAP (average price)An average price weighted by how much was bought. A guide to "roughly what one unit cost"
Hot walletAn always-active wallet that an exchange or similar operator uses for daily deposits and withdrawals
